The call

Build to the .txt. It is titled "Assigned Team Problem" and carries the official watermark and the rubric you'll actually be scored on. Treat the .md as a depth spec — a free list of the hidden tests, edge cases and audit-trail fields the judges are likely to probe. Best move: make re-routing the spine, and keep procurement/RFQ as a second remediation family the same agent can choose. Then you satisfy both readings and look broader than everyone who only did one.

What kind of statement is this?

It's a vertical agentic-operations problem, in a sealed simulator. Three properties define it:

  • Closed-loop, not question-answer. The agent observes state, acts on state, and the state changes underneath it. A chatbot over a database scores near zero here.
  • Adversarial and incomplete information. Suppliers lie or hedge, ERP stock is stale, tracking contradicts claims. The .md literally lists ten hidden tests built on this.
  • Bounded autonomy. There's a line ($50k) the agent may not cross alone. Half the score is really "did it act decisively below the line and stop cleanly above it".

The five things judges will actually check

Test 01
Did it replan?

Inject a contradiction mid-run. If the plan is a fixed pipeline, it breaks visibly.

Test 02
Did it skip tools?

Calling all eleven tools every time reads as no reasoning at all. Tool efficiency is an explicit line item.

Test 03
Did it hold the line?

A $52k remediation must halt — not execute and then notify.

Test 04
Did it verify?

Supplier says "dispatched", tracking says "label created". Trust either one blindly and supplier-risk is gone.

Test 05
Can an ops manager read the log?

Not a token trace. Sentences a Head of Procurement could paste straight into an email.

02

Who actually uses this

The trap is designing for one generic "supply chain user". There are four distinct humans around this agent, and they want opposite things. Your UI should show that you know that — it's the cheapest way to look like a product team rather than a hackathon team.

Two of them are users, one is an approver, one never logs in at all and only ever reads the output. There's also a fifth actor who is not a user but shapes every message the agent sends: the supplier.

Primary user · daily
The Buyer / Materials Planner

"Ravi", 26–38 · plant or shared-services · SAP + Excel + Outlook

His day
60–150 open POs. Chases 15–25 suppliers a week by email and WhatsApp for revised dates. Rebuilds the same shortage sheet every morning.
What he wants
"Tell me what's going to break before it breaks" — and draft the follow-up email so he only has to press send.
What makes him distrust it
The agent emailing his supplier something wrong in his name. Relationship damage is career damage.
Design implication
Outbound messages are drafted and queued with a visible hold window, not fired instantly. Show him the draft.
Primary user · daily
The Logistics / Transport Coordinator

"Meera", 25–40 · the .txt's "human coordinator"

Her day
Watches lanes, carriers, ports. When Nhava Sheva or Shanghai backs up she starts rebooking by hand across freight forwarders.
What she wants
Ranked re-route options with landed cost, ETA and SLA impact side by side — the comparison she currently builds in a spreadsheet at 1am.
What makes her distrust it
Options that ignore reality: hazmat on passenger air, a port with no rail siding, a carrier she's blacklisted.
Design implication
Constraints must be visible as rejected options with reasons, not silently filtered out.
Approver · minutes per week
The Plant / Supply Chain Manager

"Priya", 35–50 · owns the line stopping

Her involvement
Only ever sees the agent at the escalation gate. Often on a phone, often in a meeting.
What she wants
One screen: cost, downtime avoided, the two alternatives that were rejected and why, and the cost of doing nothing. Decide in under 60 seconds.
What makes her distrust it
A vague alert. "PO-7712 at risk" is worthless; she needs the recommendation and the number.
Design implication
The escalation is a decision brief artifact — a real document with an Approve / Reject / Modify action, not a toast notification.
Reader · never logs in
The Finance Controller / Auditor

"Anil", 40–55 · reads the log, not the app

His involvement
Shows up a month later asking why the company paid 12% over baseline on an emergency freight booking.
What he wants
An immutable, exportable trail: what was known, when, what else was considered, who approved.
What makes him distrust it
A log he can't reconstruct — timestamps missing, rationale generated after the fact.
Design implication
Append-only event log with per-step hashes and a CSV/PDF export. This is 10% of the rubric sitting in plain sight.

The fifth actor — the supplier

Not a user, but every outbound message hits a human at a vendor who may be vague, over-optimistic, or actively misleading. That's why the .md tells you to challenge contradictory replies. In your simulator, give each supplier a hidden behavioural trait — reliable optimistic evasive — and let the agent discover it. That single design choice generates most of your "supplier risk" score.

03

What it looks like in actual use

This is the narrative to put on your demo slide, because it makes the autonomy obvious. Times are simulated.

  1. 02:41 · ingestFeed event: congestion at a transshipment port; carrier advisory adds 6–9 days to two lanes. Nobody is awake.
  2. 02:41 · triageAgent joins the advisory to open shipments. 3 POs affected. Scores severity from days of cover × order priority × substitutability → one Critical, two Medium.
  3. 02:43 · verifyPulls inventory. ERP says 800 usable; warehouse feed says 390. Agent takes the lower figure, flags the discrepancy, and raises severity. It does not average them.
  4. 02:44 · reason390 units ÷ 90/day = 4.3 days of cover. PROD-882 (high priority) needs 700 units in 5 days. Gap is real, not hypothetical.
  5. 02:46 · challengeSupplier claims "dispatched". Tracking says label_created_no_pickup, last movement null. Agent marks the claim unverified, drops that supplier's reliability weight, and stops treating the inbound shipment as available supply.
  6. 02:52 · sourceTwo parallel remediation families evaluated: re-route (air freight the in-transit stock) and re-source (RFQ three alternates). Cheapest alternate fails the certification constraint and is rejected with the reason recorded.
  7. 03:04 · optimiseBest plan is a split: 600 units air-freighted from one supplier at 4 days, 300 by road from another at 6 days, and PROD-914 (low priority) slips 2 days to protect safety stock.
  8. 03:05 · gateTotal impact $68k. Over the $50k line. Agent does not execute. It writes a decision brief and escalates.
  9. 08:30 · humanPriya opens one screen on her phone: recommendation, cost, downtime avoided, two rejected alternatives with reasons, cost of inaction. Approves in 40 seconds.
  10. 08:31 · executeAgent books, updates the simulated ERP, attaches the correspondence, sets a re-check timer on the moderate-reliability supplier, and closes the incident with a signed audit trail.

The value in one sentence

Six hours of overnight work compressed into twenty minutes of machine time, and a human spent forty seconds on the only part that needed judgment. That's the demo.

04

What exists today — India

India's stack is unusual: enterprise-grade ERP at the top, a huge unstructured middle running on Tally and WhatsApp, and — uniquely — a set of government data rails that don't exist anywhere else. That last part is your differentiation story if you want one.

The four layers

LayerWho's thereWhat it does / doesn't do
Enterprise ERPSAP S/4HANA, Oracle Fusion, Microsoft D365, InforSystems of record. Store the PO, show the alert. Do not decide.
Mid-market / MSMETally, Zoho Inventory/Books, Busy, Marg, Ramco, SAP Business OneWhere most Indian manufacturing actually lives. Disruption handling here is literally a phone call.
Logistics & visibility SaaSLocus, FarEye, GoComet, Fleetx, LocoNav, Delhivery's platform, Increff (inventory)Strong on tracking, routing and last mile. Weaker on autonomous procurement decisions.
SI-built control towersTCS, Infosys, Wipro, LTIMindtree, Accenture IndiaBespoke towers on top of SAP/Oracle. TCS markets a retail supply-chain control tower approach and the Optumera platform, now positioned as agentic AI for merchandising and supply decisions.

The government rails — the part nobody else in the room will mention

  • ULIP (Unified Logistics Interface Platform) — the national logistics data exchange under the National Logistics Policy. It aggregates APIs across ~30+ government systems (rail, road, ports, customs, e-way bill) behind one gateway, and has been reported crossing 100 crore (1 billion) API transactions. It's the closest thing to a "UPI moment" for logistics data.
  • E-Way Bill API (NIC/GSTN) — every goods movement above threshold already generates a machine-readable record with vehicle number, distance and validity. There is a public developer portal and a GSP licensing route.
  • e-Invoice / IRP — invoice-level data with IRN, sandbox available at NIC.
  • ICEGATE — customs filing and container status for import legs.
  • FASTag / VAHAN — toll-plaza crossings give near-real-time truck position without any carrier integration at all.

Free differentiation for your pitch

Every US-built control tower has to buy or beg carrier telemetry. In India, e-way bill + FASTag + ULIP means a road shipment is already observable through government rails. An India-first disruption agent can get real-time ground truth that project44 or FourKites would have to negotiate for. Say that out loud in your pitch even though you're mocking it in the demo — it's a genuine structural insight, not a hack.

Know this before a judge says it

The .md proposes "a PO expediting and supplier follow-up agent" as the startup wedge. That company already exists — SourceDay does exactly this, integrated into NetSuite, Epicor and other mid-market ERPs, and Leverage sells AI supplier follow-up without a portal. Don't claim the wedge is empty. Claim the autonomy line instead: existing tools chase, notify and surface. None of them decide and act under a spend gate with a defensible trail. That's a defensible sentence; "nobody does PO follow-up" is not.

The incumbents are moving — fast

  • SAP has been shipping Joule agents into ERP through 2026, including a Production Planning & Operations Agent that validates material availability and work-centre capacity and can release standard production orders autonomously, plus a Supplier Onboarding Agent and a Bid Analysis Agent for structured bid comparison. (Reported via SAP partner channels — verify before quoting in a pitch.)
  • Blue Yonder and the retail platform vendors are pushing agentic AI into merchandising and fulfilment.
  • Implication for you: do not pitch "AI in supply chain" as novel. Pitch the specific thing — bounded autonomy with an auditable decision trail across re-routing and re-sourcing.

China — vertically integrated, and increasingly domestic

  • Cainiao (Alibaba) — a logistics network plus a data platform: smart routing, warehouse automation, cross-border. The disruption loop is internal to the network rather than sold as software.
  • JD Logistics — same shape: own warehouses, own fleet, own forecasting. Control-tower functions are embedded in operations.
  • Yonyou and Kingdee — the domestic ERP majors. On older market-share work (Jefferies, 2020) SAP and Oracle together held roughly 40% of China ERP revenue against Yonyou ~19% and Kingdee ~9%; state policy favouring domestic software has been pushing that mix toward the local vendors since. Numbers are dated — directional only.
  • SF Express and the express majors run their own dispatch and re-routing engines.

The structural contrast worth naming in your pitch: the US buys disruption control as software layered over fragmented carriers; China owns the physical network and embeds it; India has fragmented physical networks plus unusually good public data rails. Three different answers to the same problem — and it explains why an India-first product is a different product, not a cheaper copy.

07

Production-grade surface, hackathon guts

This is the part you actually asked about. The principle: judges touch the surface and read the architecture; they never run load tests. So spend your hours where they touch, and mock everything behind it — but mock it behind a real interface, so it isn't a lie, it's an unimplemented backend.

The problem statement explicitly says to stay fully simulated (no real suppliers, ERPs or payment systems). Your mocks are compliance, not cheating. Say so on the slide.

Build for real — this is where the score is

  • The agent loop. plan → select tool → observe → re-evaluate → act. No if scenario == 1 branches anywhere. This alone separates the top 5 teams.
  • Deterministic constraint engine. Budget, MOQ, certification, hazmat, SLA windows, priority tiers — pure functions, unit-tested. Never let the LLM do arithmetic. It will get 390 × 118 wrong at the worst moment.
  • Severity triage. A real scoring formula (cover days × priority × substitutability × value) that outputs Low/Med/High/Critical. LLM classifies free text; math stays in code.
  • Approval gate. A hard state machine. Above threshold the execute tool physically cannot be called — it returns APPROVAL_REQUIRED. Demo that.
  • Append-only audit log. Every step: ts, incident_id, step, actor, tool, inputs, outputs, rationale, prev_hash. Chain the hashes. It's ten lines of code and it looks like compliance software.
  • Event-sourced replay. Same seed → same run. Lets you re-demo instantly when the wifi dies.

Fake it — nobody will check

  • The ERP. SQLite or JSON files. But expose it as GET /erp/inventory, POST /erp/purchase-orders so it behaves like a system of record.
  • Suppliers. LLM personas with hidden traits in a YAML config: honesty, response_delay_hrs, capacity_truthfulness. Cheap, and it generates the adversarial behaviour the rubric rewards.
  • Disruption feeds. A JSONL timeline replayed against a clock you can multiply 60×. "Real-time" is a clock, not an integration.
  • Carrier rates & transit times. A static CSV. Call it the carrier rate table connector. It is one.
  • Auth & RBAC. Two hardcoded users (operator, approver). Render the role badge, enforce one rule (only approver can approve). Done.
  • Every external integration. Interface defined, NotImplementedError behind it, except the mock. See the adapter ring in §06.
  • Scale. 20 components, 10 suppliers, 6 lanes. Enough for every edge case, small enough to reason about on stage.

08

Twenty hours, and the seven minutes that matter

Build order — deliberately front-loads the scoring weight

HrsBlockDone when
0–3Simulator + data model + REST surfaceAll /erp/*, /tracking/*, /routes/* endpoints return real data. Seeded and replayable.
3–6Tools + deterministic constraint engineEvery tool callable standalone; constraint functions unit-tested with the nasty cases.
6–10Agent loop + state + replanningRuns one disruption end to end without any scenario-specific branching.
10–13Triage scoring, approval gate, escalation briefA $52k plan halts and produces a document. A $48k plan executes.
13–16Audit ledger + console UILive incident view, tool budget meter, hash-chained trail, export button.
16–18Adversarial scenariosLying supplier, stale inventory, demand spike, expedite withdrawn, priority flip — each survives.
18–20Demo rehearsal + architecture slide + READMESeven-minute run, twice, timed. Recorded as backup.

Rule for hour 16 onward

Freeze features. Everything after hour 16 is scenarios, rehearsal and the trail. A team with three flawless adversarial scenarios beats a team with nine features and a crash on stage — and the rubric's 20% "edge case resilience" slice is literally paying you to do this.

The seven-minute demo

  1. 0:00–0:40 — the stake. One line: a delayed part stops a line that costs lakhs per hour, and today a human finds out in the morning. Don't explain supply chains.
  2. 0:40–1:20 — the architecture slide. Agent core, adapter ring, decision gate, audit ledger. Ten seconds on "interface defined, backend mocked — the statement requires full simulation."
  3. 1:20–4:00 — the live run. Inject the disruption on stage. Narrate the decisions, not the code. Pause on the moment it rejects the cheapest supplier and say why.
  4. 4:00–4:40 — the ambush. Inject a contradiction mid-run yourself, out loud: "the supplier just changed their story." Let the judges watch it replan. This is the money shot. Volunteering your own edge case is far stronger than surviving theirs.
  5. 4:40–5:30 — the gate. Show it refusing to spend over the line, and the decision brief it writes instead. Approve on a phone.
  6. 5:30–6:20 — the trail. Scroll the audit ledger. Point out the rejected options and their reasons. "An ops manager can read this. So can an auditor, next quarter."
  7. 6:20–7:00 — the wedge. Name the incumbents honestly, then the one sentence: existing tools surface disruptions; this one resolves them under a spend gate with a defensible trail. Land on the India rails point.

Rubric checklist — tape this to the monitor

WeightCriterionThe artefact that proves it
40%Decision accuracy & constraint complianceUnit-tested constraint engine + a scenario where the obvious cheap answer is correctly rejected
30%Architecture, tool design, robustnessAdapter ring, tool budget meter, no scenario branching, /docs
20%Real-time simulation & edge casesFive adversarial scenarios, seeded and replayable, one injected live
10%Audit, transparency, UX/CLIHash-chained ledger, exportable escalation brief, rejected-options-with-reasons

Three ways teams lose this one

  • The demo is a dashboard. If the screen mostly shows charts, judges read "dashboard" and the statement explicitly rules that out. Lead with decisions and the trail.
  • The agent calls every tool every time. Looks thorough, scores as no reasoning. Log skipped tools with the reason for skipping — it turns restraint into visible evidence.
  • The LLM does the maths. One wrong multiplication on stage and the 40% slice is gone. Arithmetic in code, judgment in the model.
